ReadyRoute: A clear plan before. A clearer picture during.

Emergencies create two problems at once. Households need a plan they can find quickly, and responders need requests they can compare. People who face transport, language, or power barriers may be especially hard to reach. ReadyRoute is our working, offline prototype for those gaps.

It has three views. First, a household saves an out-of-area contact, meeting place, backup route, and access needs. Second, someone makes a short report with a location, need type, urgency, and barriers. A compact relay code can carry that request to another device by hand when devices cannot sync. Third, a coordinator sees an explained queue and can assign a resource.

Here is the household flow. In My plan, I enter sample details for the Rivera household: Elena as a contact, North library as a meeting point, and an accessible van as a backup. I save it locally and can print a copy. There is no account. The interface also has larger text and a partial Spanish option.

Now consider the response view. A fictional request for medical support at Pine Avenue apartments says help is needed right now and power-dependent equipment is involved. The queue shows thirty points for urgency, ten for an essential need, and six for an access barrier. ReadyRoute reserves the medical backup-power team for that request. A separate general medical team remains available for a request that does not need power support. The coordinator chooses whether to assign, which reduces capacity. The app never dispatches help on its own.

The concept could make intake faster, explain prioritization more clearly, and preserve scarce specialist support. The relay code omits free-text details but includes a location, so it should only be shared with someone trusted. It is not encrypted. We have not measured real-world impact. A real pilot would need official data, secure synchronization, consent, community testing, and emergency-management partners. This is not an official warning or dispatch system.
